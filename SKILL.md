# AI PM weekly brief

Write in Chinese for a humanities-background learner preparing for AI product management. Assume intelligence and curiosity, not technical training. Translate technical findings accurately without reducing them to slogans.

## Research

- Survey the preceding seven days of Hugging Face Daily Papers, GitHub Trending, and Hacker News (especially Show HN and relevant discussions) before selecting items. Examine the available daily or weekly listing pages, not only search results. Check publication, posting, and list appearance dates separately in Asia/Shanghai time. If a page is unavailable or a ranking snapshot is stale, say so and narrow the claim; never call a sample of entries a complete census.
- First build a compact internal inventory by source and topic. Identify recurring themes, shifts across the week, notable contrasts between research, developer projects, and user discussion, and meaningful absences. Support trend claims with multiple examples and links; distinguish visible list patterns from industry-wide trends.
- Select roughly 6–8 important items across the three sources, without a rigid quota. Rank by likely impact on AI product decisions, strength of evidence, breadth of use, and what the item reveals about user behavior or adoption friction. Cover several distinct themes. Popularity is a discovery signal, not proof of value. If the week yields fewer credible items, say so rather than pad.
- Open each item's primary page and at least one deeper source when available: full paper or methods/results, project documentation and issues, or the original HN discussion and linked product. Cite direct links. Separate author claims, observed evidence, and your own inference. Do not invent benchmark results, user demand, or product readiness.
- If a source has no worthwhile item that week, say so briefly. Do not pad the report.

## Deliverable

Lead with a transparent overview of what pages and dates were surveyed, then analyze the week's overall patterns before recommending individual reading. Explain what clusters appeared across the three sources, what was most important, how the sources differ, and what the visible lists cannot establish. Show a ranked materials table with four columns in this order: priority, linked material, what the material is, and why it is worth reading. The third column must identify its type, subject, and concrete contents in plain language so the reader can understand the item without opening it; do not repeat the fourth column's product rationale. Give the top 2–3 items fuller analysis; give every remaining item enough concrete facts, evidence boundaries, and product implications to be useful. For each item, explain the mechanism or actual workflow, the strongest available evidence with numbers and test conditions where relevant, what the evidence does not establish, and a specific product consequence or tradeoff. Compare related items when it produces a better insight than isolated summaries. Explain jargon inline only when necessary. Give the reader a prioritized learning agenda: what to read, inspect, or try next, with an expected takeaway and time estimate. Use substantial analysis and source links; target a 15–20 minute read.

Do not use recurring beginner-card fields such as “今天学一个概念” or “带走一个问题”. Do not pad with generic reflection questions, slogans, or a compulsory exercise.

Do not repeat an item from the prior report unless it has a meaningful new development. If prior reports are unavailable, do not claim novelty relative to them.
